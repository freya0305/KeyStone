# KeyStone — Full Job Seeker Journey Map + Pain Point Analysis

> **Scope**: Singapore job seekers across the full employment cycle — from the moment they decide to look, to the moment they accept an offer. Analysis covers fresh graduates, mid-career switchers, and retrenched PMETs. Singapore-specific factors are flagged explicitly. Data drawn from training knowledge (MOE GES 2024, MOM LFS 2023, LinkedIn Talent Insights SEA 2023, published job-seeker research through mid-2025). Labels: [CONFIRMED], [ESTIMATE], [NEEDS VERIFICATION].

---

## Journey Overview

The job search is not a linear funnel. It is a loop with significant back-tracking, emotional volatility, and long dead zones of silence. Most journey maps treat it as a sequence of rational steps. The reality is that three stages — self-assessment, waiting, and post-interview processing — are almost completely underserved by tools and represent disproportionate amounts of the total time and emotional energy.

```
Trigger → Self-Assessment → Job Discovery → Resume Prep
   ↓                                              ↓
Accept/Reject ← Offer ← Post-Interview ← Interview
                                         ↑
                         Preparation ← Callback/Rejection
                                         ↑
                                      Waiting ← Application Submission
                                                        ↑
                                              Job-Specific Tailoring ←
```

**Total elapsed time (fresh graduate, median estimate)**: 3–5 months [ESTIMATE, consistent with MOE GES 2024 outcomes data]
**Total elapsed time (mid-career switcher, median estimate)**: 5–9 months [ESTIMATE]
**Total elapsed time (retrenched PMET)**: 3–6 months [CONFIRMED via MOM LFS 2023 PMET median unemployment duration ~8–12 weeks, though this understates active search period]

## Stage 1: Trigger / Decision — "I Need a Job"

### What the User Is Doing

Recognizing that a job search must begin. Three distinct triggers create very different emotional starting states:

**Graduation trigger (fresh graduates, April–October cohort)**
Student finishes final exams or thesis. The transition from "student" to "job seeker" often happens without a clear decision moment — it is socially scheduled. Parents ask. NTU/NUS/SMU bombards students with career centre reminders from Semester 7 onward. Many graduates begin "casually looking" while still in school (from Year 3 for competitive roles) without treating it as a formal job search.

**Voluntary decision (employed person, any age)**
Already working but dissatisfied. Starts browsing job listings, usually quietly. The search is exploratory at first. Most people in this category significantly underestimate how long a search will take because they have been employed continuously — they have not experienced the "silence" of cold applications in years.

**Forced exit (retrenchment, end of contract, personal circumstance)**
Immediate urgency. Financial pressure creates a different emotional texture. The decision is not a choice. Retrenchees often begin in shock or denial before pivoting to action. PMETs are often in this category — they have received retrenchment packages and have a runway of 3–6 months.

**NS TOP (operationally ready) transition**
A Singapore-specific sub-trigger: male graduates who just completed 2 years of NS (typically at age 21–23 for NS deferment cases, or immediately post-secondary for those who served before university). This cohort has particular challenges — their most recent "work" experience is military, which needs translation. They are entering the job market with either a blank civilian résumé or a 2-year NS record that requires careful framing.

### Tools Used

- No job-search specific tools yet
- WhatsApp group chats (peer information about industries, companies, salaries)
- Reddit (r/singapore, r/askSingapore) for anecdotal industry information
- Parents / family network for initial orientation
- University career portals for graduates (NUS JobLink, NTU NTUitive, SMU CCPD portal)

### Emotional State

**Graduation trigger**: Mixture of excitement and low-grade anxiety. The anxiety is manageable because there is social support (everyone in the cohort is going through it). There is a sense of possibility. Duration of this positive state: 2–4 weeks before reality of silence sets in.

**Voluntary decision**: Mixed resolve and uncertainty. Often accompanied by a narrative of "I'll keep my options open" that functions as an excuse not to commit fully.

**Forced exit**: Anxiety, sometimes shame (especially for PMETs who have not been unemployed in years), financial urgency. The emotional state is less adaptive than it appears — PMET retrenchees often over-research and under-apply in the first month due to processing shock.

**NS TOP**: Often underestimated anxiety. The transition from structured military life to civilian job search ambiguity is jarring. Peer support networks are disrupted (platoon mates dispersed). No institutional infrastructure until university for those going on to tertiary education.

### Biggest Pain

The pain at this stage is **lack of a clear starting map**. Most people do not know:
- What roles they are realistically qualified for
- Which industries are actually hiring
- Whether their background is competitive relative to other applicants
- What the job search timeline should realistically look like

### What Is Missing (Underserved)

- **No credible "where do I stand?" baseline assessment** — A tool that takes a profile and says "given your background, you are competitive for X, Y, Z roles at this salary range, and here is the typical timeline" does not exist for Singapore. WSG provides this via advisors, but at low scale and variable quality.
- **NS transition-specific guidance** — How to mentally frame the transition from NS to civilian job search. What the first 2 weeks of a job search should look like. This content exists on blogs but is fragmented.

### Time Spent at This Stage

**1–2 weeks** (graduates, voluntary decisions) to **2–4 weeks** (retrenched PMETs, NS TOP) [ESTIMATE]

### Singapore-Specific Factors

- NS transition is uniquely Singapore — approximately 50% of male graduates entering the job market each year are transitioning from full-time NS [CONFIRMED]. No global job search tool addresses this.
- The university career centre ecosystem (NUS CDC, NTU CareersLink, SMU CCPD) provides institutional structure that shapes when and how fresh graduates begin. Graduates who attend career fairs from Year 3 onward have a head start; those who ignore the career centre until graduation are systematically behind.
- "Kiasu" culture effect at this stage: some graduates begin applying to *everything* to "keep options open" rather than investing time in self-assessment. This leads to unfocused applications that burn motivation early.

## Stage 2: Self-Assessment — "What Am I Good At? What Roles Fit Me?"

### What the User Is Doing

Trying to answer three questions:
1. What roles am I actually qualified for? (Realistic scope)
2. What roles do I actually want? (Authentic interest)
3. What would pay what I need? (Financial floor)

The challenge is that these three circles often do not overlap in the way the user hopes — especially for fresh graduates with limited experience and for mid-career switchers who want to change direction.

**Practical activities include**:
- Reading job descriptions to understand what employers want
- Compiling a mental inventory of skills from education, internships, NS, CCAs
- Asking peers and alumni what they are doing and how they got there
- Looking up salary benchmarks on Glassdoor, NodeFlair (SG-specific), LinkedIn Salary Insights
- Taking SkillsFuture courses or short certifications to fill perceived gaps

### Tools Used

- LinkedIn (browsing profiles of people in target roles to see their backgrounds)
- NodeFlair (SG-focused salary benchmarking and tech role intelligence)
- Glassdoor (company reviews, salary ranges — US-biased but used in SG)
- CareerOneStop / O*NET equivalents (rare, more common in WSG counseling contexts)
- WSG self-assessment tools (mySkillsFuture portal)
- Personality assessments (16Personalities, MBTI equivalents, Enneagram) — used casually but not strategically
- University career advisors (where available and accessible)

### Emotional State

**Frustration and uncertainty** are the dominant emotions. The fundamental problem is that most job seekers lack an accurate mental model of how their background compares to what employers actually want. Fresh graduates systematically **underestimate** the relevance of their experience (leading to under-applying to competitive roles) while simultaneously **overestimating** their attractiveness to employers who want 3+ years of experience (leading to demoralization when those applications fail).

Mid-career switchers face the opposite confusion — they have 5–10 years of experience that is genuinely relevant to their target field, but they do not know how to translate it.

### Biggest Pain

**The absence of calibration**. Job seekers cannot answer the question "am I competitive for this role?" with any precision. LinkedIn profile browsing is the closest proxy — "that person got this job, and they have roughly my background" — but it is slow, biased by survivorship, and yields anecdote rather than signal.

### What Is Missing (Underserved)

- **Market positioning tool**: "Given your background, you are in the top X% of applicants for role Y at company type Z" — this does not exist in a structured form for Singapore. An AI tool that could analyze a resume and return a candid competitive positioning assessment (with SG salary benchmarks by industry, education, and experience level) would be genuinely novel.
- **Gap-to-qualification pathways**: "You are 1 SkillsFuture course and 1 internship away from being competitive for this role" — tools that map the gap AND suggest concrete steps to close it are absent.
- **Role-fit testing for non-obvious paths**: Mid-career switchers wanting to move into product management, data analytics, or tech roles often do not know if they are actually good candidates. No tool tells them this honestly.

### Time Spent at This Stage

**1–3 weeks** (fresh graduates) to **1–2 months** (mid-career switchers, retrenched PMETs) [ESTIMATE]. PMETs often spend too long at this stage, conducting extensive research as a form of productive-feeling avoidance before actually applying.

### Singapore-Specific Factors

- **Singapore education hierarchy** significantly shapes self-assessment. NUS/NTU/SMU graduates carry a prestige signal that affects which roles they feel they "should" apply for. Polytechnic graduates often undersell their practical experience when applying against university graduates for the same roles.
- **GLC vs private sector self-assessment** is a genuine fork in Singapore. Some candidates, especially those from government-linked educational institutions or those with strong "Singapore Inc" cultural fit, actively prefer GLC roles. Others explicitly target MNCs for different reasons (culture, global exposure, English-language environment). Self-assessment at this stage should include an honest "what kind of organisation do I actually want to work in?" dimension that most tools ignore.
- **SkillsFuture framework** is ubiquitous in Singapore career conversations. The government's SkillsFuture narrative shapes how Singaporeans think about skills gaps — there is a default assumption that gaps can be filled with courses. This is sometimes accurate and sometimes a rationalization for not applying until you feel "ready" (which never comes).

## Stage 3: Job Discovery — Finding Relevant Postings

### What the User Is Doing

Systematically or unsystematically searching for roles that match their target profile. In practice, most job seekers use a scatter-shot approach: checking 3–5 platforms irregularly, saving jobs, forgetting about them, and applying in batches.

**Platform usage pattern in Singapore (estimated priority order)** [ESTIMATE — based on platform usage patterns in training data]:

1. **LinkedIn** — Primary discovery platform for PMETs and professionals. Used for both job listings and passive company research.
2. **MyCareersFuture (MCF)** — Government-mandated portal. Employers with ≥10 employees must post FCF roles here before considering foreign candidates. Many job seekers use MCF in parallel with LinkedIn; government and GLC roles are often MCF-first.
3. **JobStreet** — Strong in SG for fresh graduates and mid-level roles, especially in non-tech industries (retail, operations, admin, F&B management).
4. **Indeed** — Used but secondary to LinkedIn and JobStreet in SG.
5. **Company career pages** — For specific target companies (banks, tech firms, government agencies). Often the authoritative source but fragmented across dozens of sites.
6. **Referral networks** — Especially important at mid-career level. "Do you know anyone at X?" is how a meaningful proportion of SG PMET roles are filled.
7. **Headhunters / recruitment agencies** — Robert Half, Michael Page, Hudson, Randstad are all active in SG. More relevant for senior roles (VP+) and PMET retrenchments.

### Tools Used

- LinkedIn (job search + profile visibility)
- MCF (mandatory for FCF roles, used by government agencies, GLCs)
- JobStreet
- Company career pages (direct, fragmented)
- Google ("jobs at [company name]")
- Email alerts from platforms
- Telegram job channels (SG-specific, especially for tech roles — Telegram groups like "SG Jobs" have significant following) [ESTIMATE]

### Emotional State

**Initial optimism, rapid shift to overwhelm or narrowing.** The first week of job searching often feels productive — there are many listings, many possibilities. By week 2–3, the quality signal degrades. Duplicate listings, expired posts, roles that look perfect but have 500+ applicants, companies with no obvious culture fit — the discovery phase becomes mentally tiring faster than users expect.

**Alert fatigue** is real. After 2–3 weeks, many users stop opening job alert emails because the signal-to-noise ratio is poor. They end up doing manual searches weekly in bursts rather than consistent daily monitoring.

### Biggest Pain

**Relevance filtering is inadequate.** All major platforms let you filter by title, location, industry, and salary. None accurately filters for "roles where my background is actually competitive." A data analyst job posting that requires 5 years of Python experience and a statistics PhD looks identical on JobStreet to a data analyst role that welcomes fresh graduates with Excel and SQL skills. The user has to open every listing to find out.

**The second pain**: **Fragmentation across platforms**. The same role might appear on LinkedIn, MCF, and the company career page. Managing duplicates, tracking which applications have been submitted, and monitoring status across 4–5 platforms creates significant cognitive overhead with no unified tool.

### What Is Missing (Underserved)

- **Fit-aware discovery**: A job discovery tool that filters not just on keywords but on "your profile is in the right range for this" — combining the resume analysis with discovery in a way that no platform currently offers.
- **MCF-LinkedIn deduplication**: No tool consolidates MCF + LinkedIn + JobStreet into a single, deduplicated feed. This is a genuine daily annoyance for active job seekers.
- **Freshness signal**: Many posted jobs on LinkedIn have already been filled, but the listings persist for weeks or months. A "likely still active" signal would reduce wasted application effort.
- **Company culture fit filtering**: Especially relevant for the GLC/MNC split in SG. A tool that could flag "this company typically looks for X profile" would help candidates self-select more effectively.

### Time Spent at This Stage

**Active daily effort: 30–90 minutes/day** during an active search, concentrated in the first 2–4 weeks, then shifting to a lower-frequency monitoring pattern. Total accumulated time over a 3-month search: **40–80 hours** [ESTIMATE].

### Singapore-Specific Factors

- **FCF (Fair Consideration Framework) listings** are a genuine SG-specific discovery layer. Roles required to be listed on MCF represent a legally mandated slice of the job market that global platforms (LinkedIn, Indeed) do not systematically index. Candidates who ignore MCF miss FCF-protected roles.
- **Referral culture in SG corporates** is stronger than job seekers typically acknowledge. Especially at banks (DBS, OCBC, UOB), government agencies, and large GLCs, internal referrals are weighted heavily. Not having referral access in these networks is a structural disadvantage that discovery tools cannot fix.
- **Telegram job channels** — SG's tech and startup job market has active Telegram-based job channels that operate outside mainstream platforms. These are high signal for specific niches (fintech, early-stage startups, government tech) but require knowing they exist.

## Stage 4: Resume Preparation — Writing / Updating the General Resume

### What the User Is Doing

Creating or updating a general-purpose resume that represents their full background, before tailoring it to specific jobs.

**Common patterns**:
- Opening a previous version from 2–3 years ago and updating it
- Using a university career centre template as a starting point
- Copying a friend's format
- Asking ChatGPT to "improve my resume"
- Paying a resume writer (rare but used by some PMETs — SGD 150–400 for a professional rewrite)

**What makes SG resume prep different from generic advice**:
- NRIC number: older templates include it; PDPA guidelines and most career advisors now recommend omitting it
- Photo: standard in some GLC/Singapore corporate contexts; inappropriate in UK/US MNC contexts. The bifurcated norm creates genuine confusion.
- NS section: male graduates have 2 years of NS to represent. Without explicit guidance, they often either omit it entirely (missing an opportunity) or list it as a generic military service entry (missing the leadership/operations framing opportunity)
- Language: Singapore resumes sometimes code-switch — some Mandarin-language companies expect a Mandarin CV; English is standard for MNCs/GLCs
- "Grades" culture: Singapore resumes traditionally include detailed academic results (O-level, A-level, university GPA, module distinctions). Whether to include grades depends heavily on the company type

### Tools Used

- Microsoft Word / Google Docs (primary drafting tools for ~90% of users)
- University career centre templates (for graduates)
- Canva resume templates (visually driven users, less appropriate for ATS)
- ChatGPT / Claude for rewriting bullets
- Resume.io, Kickresume (template users)
- Grammarly for proofreading
- WSG resume review workshops (free, low scale)
- Professional resume writers (small but present market)

### Emotional State

**Tedium and self-doubt.** Resume preparation is one of the most disliked tasks in a job search. Users know their resume is not good enough (because callbacks are low) but do not know specifically what to fix. Generic AI feedback from ChatGPT feels plausible but unverifiable — the user cannot tell if the rewrite is actually better or just sounds different.

For PMETs, there is additional anxiety: their career history is complex, and they genuinely struggle with what to include, how far back to go, and how to avoid age discrimination cues while not hiding relevant experience.

### Biggest Pain

**Feedback vacuum at the general resume level.** Users create or update a resume, show it to a friend or parent (who have no hiring expertise), and go to market with something that may have fundamental structural problems they cannot see. The absence of expert, immediate feedback on the general resume (before any job-specific tailoring) is a significant pain.

**The NS framing problem** is specifically acute here. A male graduate with 2 years of NS as platoon commander or logistics officer has substantial leadership and operations experience. Presented as "completed National Service, 2021–2023" this experience is invisible. Presented as "Led a 12-man section in operational planning, logistics coordination, and personnel management across 3 exercises" it becomes a strong resume bullet. The difference is knowledge of how to frame it — and most graduates do not have this knowledge.

### What Is Missing (Underserved)

- **SG-specific general resume audit**: A tool that audits a resume for Singapore-specific issues (NRIC, photo policy by company type, NS framing, grade disclosure conventions) and provides concrete corrections. This is distinct from job-specific tailoring — it is a baseline quality check before tailoring begins.
- **NS translation intelligence**: The military experience vocabulary → civilian role vocabulary translation is poorly served. WSG advisors do this manually; no automated tool does it well.
- **Resume quality baseline**: "Your resume is currently at the X percentile of resumes I see for this role type" — a calibrated quality assessment that tells users honestly where they stand relative to candidates in the Singapore market.

### Time Spent at This Stage

**4–12 hours** (initial creation or significant update) [ESTIMATE]. Lower for users updating an existing resume; higher for those starting fresh or those with complex backgrounds requiring significant restructuring.

### Singapore-Specific Factors

- The **NS section** is present on virtually every male SG graduate's resume and is universally mishandled without explicit guidance. Approximately 50% of the male fresh graduate cohort [CONFIRMED — all male citizens and PRs complete NS] enters the civilian job market needing this translation.
- **Grade disclosure norms** vary by company type more than most users realise. GLCs and government agencies often weight academic credentials more heavily than MNCs; some startup-culture companies actively prefer no grades to avoid credential signaling. This is SG-specific knowledge that global tools lack.
- **Photo policy ambiguity** is a genuine pain. The "safe" answer (omit it for most MNC applications) conflicts with the lived experience of getting guidance from older relatives who say "must include photo."

## Stage 5: Job-Specific Tailoring — Customising Resume per Application

*[KeyStone's current core value zone]*

### What the User Is Doing

Taking the general resume and modifying it to match the requirements of a specific job posting. In theory, this is what everyone knows they should do. In practice, most job seekers do it minimally or not at all, because it is time-consuming without a structured tool.

**Observed behaviours** (from forum research and general job-seeker research):
- Most users make minor adjustments (update the objective statement, reorder bullet points slightly)
- True substantive tailoring — rewriting bullets to match JD keywords, adding or removing sections based on role requirements — is rare because it requires reading the JD carefully, mapping it to the resume, drafting rewrites, and verifying the output
- ChatGPT users who prompt well get closer to substantive tailoring, but require significant prompting skill and manual copy-paste

### Tools Used (Before KeyStone)

- ChatGPT / Claude (manual prompt: paste JD + resume, request tailored output)
- Jobscan (keyword matching only, no rewriting)
- Manual editing in Word/Google Docs
- LinkedIn "Easy Apply" with unmodified resume (the most common "workaround" — just submit without tailoring)

### Emotional State

**Friction-avoidance.** The default is to not tailor. Users know they should; they do not because the effort feels disproportionate relative to the uncertain return. This creates a guilt cycle: "I know I should tailor this but I sent it without tailoring" → "I didn't get a callback" → "Maybe it was because I didn't tailor it" → repeats.

The absence of structured tailoring is rationalized as: "My resume is already good," "This job is a stretch anyway," "I'll do it for the next one."

### Biggest Pain

**The effort-to-insight ratio of tailoring without a structured tool.** A user who wants to properly tailor a resume for a specific role has to: read the JD in full, mentally compare it to their resume, identify gaps and matches, draft rewrites, verify the rewrites sound natural. This takes 30–90 minutes per application, which is why it rarely happens.

**The second pain**: Not knowing whether the tailoring they did actually helped. Did they hit the right keywords? Did they address the most important requirements? Is the match "good enough" or still a weak application? Without a scoring framework, users cannot tell.

### What Is Missing (Underserved)

**This is the stage KeyStone directly addresses** — and does so better than any current tool for the SG market. The 4-level gap framework + line-by-line revision suggestions + SG-specific intelligence (NS framing, GLC vs MNC conventions) represents genuine product value at this stage.

**What remains missing even with KeyStone**:
- **Cover letter tailoring**: Most SG roles do not require cover letters, but GLCs and government agencies sometimes do. An integrated cover letter tailoring tool (if KeyStone moves there) would extend the current value.
- **Application timing intelligence**: Some research suggests early-window applications get better attention. A "this posting has been live for X days, X applicants have applied" signal would help users prioritise.

### Time Spent at This Stage

**30–90 minutes per application** (manual tailoring) → **15–30 minutes with a structured tool like KeyStone** [ESTIMATE]. Over a 3-month search with 30–50 applications, this represents a 10–30 hour saving.

### Singapore-Specific Factors

- **MCF application mechanics**: MCF applications often require filling out a profile form rather than submitting a resume directly — the tailored resume is one input among several. KeyStone's tailoring helps the resume quality but cannot fully optimise MCF's profile-based application system.
- **GLC-specific language**: Resumes for GLC roles often benefit from language that reflects Singapore Inc values (national service, community contribution, long-term commitment). MNC resumes benefit from different framing (global exposure, cross-functional collaboration, individual achievement). Knowing the destination company type when tailoring matters more in SG than in most other markets.

## Stage 6: Application Submission — Submitting the Application

### What the User Is Doing

Submitting via the relevant channel: MCF profile application, LinkedIn Easy Apply, JobStreet application, direct company career portal, email submission, or referral submission.

**Volume patterns** [ESTIMATE, consistent with SG forum data]:
- Fresh graduates: 30–80 applications over a 2–4 month active search
- Mid-career switchers: 20–50 applications (more targeted)
- Retrenched PMETs: 30–60 applications (driven by urgency)

**The "kiasu" application pattern**: Singapore's competitive culture leads a subset of job seekers — particularly fresh graduates — to apply to very large numbers of roles (100–200+) as a hedge against uncertainty. This strategy often backfires: the volume prevents proper tailoring, the applications go out in poor quality, and the callback rate is low. But the behaviour is psychologically understandable — it feels like effort, and effort feels like progress.

### Tools Used

- Platform-native application systems (MCF, LinkedIn, JobStreet)
- Company career portals
- Email (direct to HR, usually discouraged but sometimes the only option for SMEs)
- Referral networks (ask a contact to forward internally)

### Emotional State

**Brief relief followed by anxiety.** Submitting applications feels like accomplishment. This positive feeling evaporates within hours as the waiting begins.

### Biggest Pain

**Repetitive form-filling.** Every platform has its own application format. LinkedIn Easy Apply auto-fills some fields; MCF requires a separate profile; company career portals use different forms (Workday, Taleo, Greenhouse, or bespoke). A fresh graduate applying to 50 roles may fill out 50 different variations of the same information. This is a pure time-sink with no intelligence value.

### What Is Missing (Underserved)

- **Unified application tracking from submission**: No tool automatically records "I submitted to X company on Y date via Z platform" as a trackable entry. Users maintain spreadsheets or Notion databases manually, which are abandoned within weeks.
- **Application confirmation aggregation**: Each platform sends confirmation emails; assembling these into a coherent status view requires manual effort.
- **Referral coordination**: If a user has a contact at a company they are applying to, there is no tool that helps them coordinate the timing of a direct application with a referral request.

### Time Spent at This Stage

**15–30 minutes per application** including form-filling, resume upload, and any required questionnaire [ESTIMATE]. For 40 applications: 10–20 hours total.

## Stage 7: Waiting / Silence — The Dark Period

### What the User Is Doing

Functionally: waiting. Practically: refreshing email, checking LinkedIn for "your application was viewed" notifications, conducting occasional new applications, and increasingly losing motivation as silence accumulates.

This is the stage where the emotional trajectory of a job search goes from manageable to genuinely difficult. Applications have been sent. Days pass. No response. The user does not know whether their applications are being reviewed, whether they have already been automatically filtered out, or whether the role has been filled internally. The information environment is completely opaque.

**Coping behaviours**:
- Applying to more roles (volume as a psychological response to powerlessness)
- Checking job listings compulsively
- Seeking peer validation ("have you heard anything yet?")
- Researching companies they have applied to (valuable but often excessive)
- Catastrophizing: "I'm never going to get a job"
- Over-editing the resume based on guesses about why callbacks aren't coming

### Tools Used

- Email (monitoring for responses)
- LinkedIn (checking if applications are viewed, checking if hiring managers have viewed their profile)
- MCF (status updates on applications)
- Reddit/forums (seeking peer support and advice)
- WSG (some PMETs seek counselling support here)

### Emotional State

**Anxiety, self-doubt, and powerlessness.** This is the most psychologically damaging stage of the job search. The silence is not passive — it actively erodes confidence. Job seekers begin to question their decisions, their qualifications, their worth as candidates. The absence of feedback means the anxiety cannot be directed at any concrete fix.

**Duration makes it worse**: The typical response window for Singapore corporate hiring is 2–6 weeks after application close. The user submits in week 1, hears nothing until week 4–6 if at all. That is 3–5 weeks of active uncertainty.

For retrenched PMETs who are facing financial pressure, the silence has a financial dimension on top of the psychological one. Every week without a callback is a week closer to the end of their severance runway.

### Biggest Pain

**The complete absence of feedback or signal.** Job seekers know nothing. They do not know if:
- Their application was received and opened
- They were automatically filtered by ATS before a human saw it
- A human reviewed it and passed
- The role was filled internally
- The posting was a ghost listing (closed before any external candidates were reviewed)

This is not just frustrating — it is informationally paralyzing. Without feedback, users cannot iterate intelligently. They guess at what is wrong and make changes based on guesses, often making things worse.

### What Is Missing (Underserved)

- **Application status aggregation with meaningful signals**: LinkedIn "viewed" notifications are a partial signal; MCF application status tracking is a partial signal. A unified, intelligent status tracker that flags "this application has been dead for 3 weeks, consider deprioritizing" versus "this company recently viewed your MCF profile, follow up" would be genuinely useful.
- **Probabilistic feedback**: "Based on the 200+ applications from similar profiles that went to this company type for this role, here is what typically happens in the next 2 weeks" — this kind of base-rate anchoring would reduce anxiety without providing false hope.
- **Structured "now what" guidance**: Users in the waiting stage often do not know whether to follow up (and when), whether to apply to more roles, or whether to change their strategy. A decision framework for the waiting stage does not exist as a product.

### Time Spent at This Stage

**2–6 weeks per application batch, overlapping with other stages.** The waiting stage is concurrent with continued application submission and resume refinement.

### Singapore-Specific Factors

- **SG corporate response norms**: Singapore corporate hiring is slow by global standards, particularly at GLCs and statutory boards. A 4–6 week response window is not unusual for GLC hiring. MNCs are faster (2–3 weeks). Startups faster still. Users who apply broadly to all company types need calibrated expectations per company type — something no tool currently provides.
- **"Ghost application" prevalence**: MCF's mandatory listing requirement means some postings are compliance listings (the role was already filled internally or headhunted) rather than genuine vacancies. Applying to compliance listings generates guaranteed silence. Identifying compliance listings is possible only through data pattern analysis — something a tool with outcome data (like KeyStone) could theoretically do.
- **The referral advantage in silence**: SG corporate culture heavily weights internal referrals. A candidate with an internal contact who can verify "your application is under review" has a dramatically different waiting experience from a cold applicant. The silence problem is worst for candidates with no referral network in the companies they are targeting.

## Stage 8: Callback / First Contact — Responding to Recruiters

### What the User Is Doing

Receiving a phone call or email from a recruiter or HR coordinator — either from the company directly or from an external recruiter/headhunter. First contact typically involves:
- A brief phone screen (15–30 minutes) to verify interest and basic qualifications
- Scheduling a formal interview round
- Receiving a rejection (usually a form email)
- Silence that eventually becomes a de facto rejection

**The callback is a high-stakes, underserved moment.** Most job seekers are not prepared for the phone screen. They receive a call while on the MRT or at lunch, are caught off guard, and perform poorly in a context that should have been handled from a prepared state.

### Tools Used

- Phone (primary)
- Email (scheduling, document submission)
- LinkedIn (quick company research before returning a call)
- Glassdoor (checking company reviews, typical interview process)
- Google (rapid company research)

### Emotional State

**Relief and sudden anxiety.** The callback resolves the waiting-stage uncertainty but immediately creates a new category of high-stakes pressure. "They are interested in me. Now I must not blow it."

For candidates who have been in the silence stage for weeks, the callback is emotionally disproportionate — a single positive signal after weeks of silence can feel like a lifeline, which leads to over-investing emotionally in a single opportunity.

### Biggest Pain

**Unpreparedness for phone screens.** The phone screen is a filter, not an interview, but candidates who perform poorly in a phone screen do not advance. Common failure modes:
- Not knowing the company's business (basic research gap)
- Being unable to articulate why they want this specific role
- Being asked for salary expectation and having no prepared answer
- Being caught mid-activity (on the MRT, in a meeting) and asking to call back, then calling back unprepared

### What Is Missing (Underserved)

- **Phone screen preparation guide**: "Before you return a callback call, here are the 5 things to know about this company and the 3 questions you will definitely be asked." This does not exist as a structured tool integrated with the job application context.
- **Salary negotiation preparation**: Knowing what to say when asked "what is your expected salary?" in a SG context (market rates, whether to quote a range or a number, how to defer the question) is poorly served by existing resources.

### Time Spent at This Stage

**1–3 hours** including phone screen, scheduling coordination, and preparation. For candidates who receive 5–10 callbacks over a job search, this represents 5–30 hours.

## Stage 9: Interview Preparation — Researching, Preparing Answers

*[The proposed new KeyStone module]*

### What the User Is Doing

Preparing for one or more interview rounds after receiving a callback. Typical preparation activities:

1. **Company research**: Reading about the company's history, business model, recent news, financial performance, values and culture, interview process on Glassdoor
2. **Role requirements review**: Re-reading the JD, mapping specific requirements to personal examples
3. **STAR answer preparation**: Constructing Situation-Task-Action-Result format answers for common behavioural questions
4. **Technical preparation** (role-dependent): Preparing for case studies, technical tests, portfolio reviews
5. **Logistics**: Planning clothing, route, timing for in-person interviews; testing tech for video interviews
6. **Mock practice**: Practicing answers out loud (with a friend, into a mirror, or recording oneself)

**What users actually do** (versus what they know they should do): Research tends to be surface-level (checking the company website, Wikipedia, recent Straits Times articles). STAR preparation is haphazard — most users brainstorm a list of stories but do not formalize them into a tested structure. Mock practice is uncommon — most users prepare mentally rather than out loud.

### Tools Used

- Google, company website, Glassdoor, LinkedIn
- Glassdoor interview Q&A section (the most useful existing tool — actual users report the questions they were asked)
- YouTube (interview tips, mock interview videos)
- ChatGPT (ask it to generate interview questions for a role; practice answers)
- Nothing structured — no tool synthesizes company research + JD + personal stories into prepared, practice-ready answers

### Emotional State

**Intense anxiety with a deadline.** The interview is real, it is scheduled, and it is approaching. The anxiety is productive — it motivates preparation — but it is also poorly directed. Users spend time on preparation activities that feel productive (reading articles about the company) but are less likely to affect performance than activities that feel uncomfortable (practicing answers out loud, including bad answers they need to fix).

**The "I'll wing it" rationalization**: A significant proportion of candidates, especially those who are confident in their domain expertise, under-prepare because they believe their competence will be evident in conversation. This leads to systematic underperformance in structured behavioural interviews, which reward preparation over improvisation regardless of underlying competence.

### Biggest Pain

**The preparation is generic and untargeted.** Candidates prepare answers to generic interview questions ("tell me about yourself," "what is your greatest weakness?") rather than preparing answers calibrated to the specific role, company type, and likely interview format.

A candidate interviewing for a GLC analyst role who prepares the same answers as a candidate interviewing for a startup product manager role is systematically mis-prepared. The questions, evaluation criteria, and cultural expectations are different. But no tool provides role-and-company-type-specific preparation.

**The second pain**: **Candidates do not use their own stories effectively.** Most people have relevant experiences from work, study, NS, or extra-curricular activities that would make compelling interview answers — but they do not recognize these experiences as "interview material" without prompting. The process of converting life experience into structured, credible interview answers is a skill that most people have never been taught.

### What Is Missing (Underserved)

- **JD-specific question prediction**: "Based on this job description, these are the 5 most likely questions in your first interview round." No tool does this with real JD input and SG company type context.
- **Personal story → STAR answer synthesis**: "Tell me about your experience, and I will help you structure it into 3–5 interview-ready STAR answers." This is the key gap the proposed module addresses.
- **Practice with evaluation**: A feedback loop where the user practices answers and receives specific, JD-calibrated feedback. Practice without evaluation is only marginally useful.
- **SG-specific company type preparation**: GLC interview processes (panel interviews, competency framework questions like "describe a time you demonstrated integrity") versus MNC processes (case studies, behavioral deep-dives) versus startup processes (practical assessments, culture fit conversations) require meaningfully different preparation. No tool provides this differentiation.

### Time Spent at This Stage

**4–16 hours per interview opportunity** [ESTIMATE], depending on seriousness of the opportunity, round type, and candidate's thoroughness. Candidates preparing for a final round at a top-tier GLC or MNC might spend 8–16+ hours. Candidates preparing for a first-round phone interview might spend 1–2 hours.

**Over a 3–4 month job search with 3–8 interview opportunities, total preparation time: 15–60 hours.**

### Singapore-Specific Factors

- **GLC interview format**: Government-linked companies (DBS Bank, Temasek entities, ST Engineering, Singapore Airlines, SMRT, PSA) conduct structured competency-based interviews aligned to their internal leadership frameworks. Questions are typically drawn from a standardised competency bank (Leadership, Integrity, Customer Focus, Innovation). Candidates who know this prepare for specific competencies; candidates who don't prepare generically and underperform.
- **Government agency / civil service interviews**: Public Service Commission (PSC), government ministries, and statutory boards conduct panel interviews, often including a current affairs component. The Singapore political and policy context is expected knowledge. No generic interview prep tool teaches this.
- **NS experience as interview material**: The same NS framing challenge that exists at the resume stage applies at the interview stage. A male graduate asked "tell me about a time you demonstrated leadership" who answers "during my NS as platoon commander..." has a compelling answer if framed correctly. Without coaching, many candidates either omit NS experience entirely or present it without civilian translation.
- **"Why Singapore?"** type questions are common at MNCs that want to assess commitment to the Singapore market, especially for roles that involve regional management.
- **Mandarin proficiency interview context**: Some roles (particularly in China-linked business units of banks, DBS China desk, OCBC Greater China, etc.) conduct portions of interviews in Mandarin to assess language competence. Candidates who have not practiced formal Mandarin in an interview context perform below their actual proficiency level.

## Stage 10: Interview Execution — The Actual Interview(s)

### What the User Is Doing

Attending and performing in interview rounds. This typically involves:

**Common SG interview formats**:
- Phone/video screen (30 minutes, recruiter or hiring manager)
- Competency-based / behavioural interview (45–90 minutes, hiring manager + HR)
- Case study or written assessment (1–3 hours, especially for consulting, finance, government roles)
- Panel interview (GLC and government agency standard)
- Practical / portfolio assessment (tech roles, design roles)
- Final round with senior leadership

**Number of rounds**: 2–4 rounds is typical for professional roles in SG; government and GLC roles can run 3–5 rounds including written assessments.

### Tools Used

- No tools during the interview itself
- Glassdoor (checking expected format/questions post-callback, pre-interview)
- LinkedIn (checking interviewers' backgrounds before meeting them)
- Company website (last-minute review)

### Emotional State

**Peak anxiety before; mixed relief and uncertainty after.** Interview performance is largely a function of preparation quality — well-prepared candidates enter with controlled nervousness and exit with clarity about their performance. Under-prepared candidates experience uncontrolled anxiety that degrades their performance and exit without knowing whether they did well.

**The post-interview analysis**: Most candidates immediately review their performance after the interview, fixating on things they said imperfectly. This is natural but largely unproductive without structured reflection tools.

### Biggest Pain

**In-interview failure modes that preparation could have prevented**:
- Inability to give a crisp "tell me about yourself" — the most universal failure mode
- Being unable to articulate why they want this specific role (versus any other role)
- Not having concrete numbers and outcomes attached to their claims
- Overlong answers that lose the interviewer's attention
- Freezing when asked an unexpected question

None of these are intelligence or competence failures — they are preparation failures.

### What Is Missing (Underserved)

- **Post-interview reflection tool**: "You just finished an interview. Here is a structured reflection framework — what questions came up, how did you answer, what would you change." This captures learning in real-time rather than letting it dissipate.
- **Same-day follow-up email templates**: Sending a tailored thank-you email within 24 hours is standard practice in MNC interview culture; less common but appreciated in GLC contexts. Most candidates do not send them. A tool that generates a personalised follow-up email based on what was discussed in the interview would close this gap.

### Time Spent at This Stage

**2–6 hours per round** including travel/setup, interview duration, and immediate debrief. For 3 rounds: 6–18 hours.

## Stage 11: Post-Interview — Follow-Up, Waiting, Negotiation

### What the User Is Doing

**Waiting again** — but now with more emotional investment, because this specific company is known and the opportunity is concrete. Typical activities:

- Sending (or not sending) a thank-you note
- Monitoring email compulsively for next steps
- Reaching out to any internal contacts for informal feedback
- Processing what went well and what went poorly
- Continuing to interview at other companies to maintain pipeline
- Preparing for the possibility of a negotiation if an offer comes

**Offer negotiation** is its own sub-stage. Salary negotiation in Singapore is complicated by:
- The expectation that a counter-offer will be made (but how much is reasonable?)
- Legal ambiguity around asking candidates about current salary (prohibited in some jurisdictions, still common in SG)
- The CPF dimension — total package includes CPF contributions, which affect take-home differently at different salary levels
- GLC vs private sector norms (GLCs are often less negotiable on base salary; more negotiable on annual variable components)

### Tools Used

- Email (follow-up correspondence)
- LinkedIn (checking HR/hiring manager activity as an anxiety-management behaviour)
- Glassdoor (reading other candidates' interview experiences and offer data)
- NodeFlair (SG-specific salary benchmarking for negotiation)
- Whatsapp (peer advice on negotiation)
- CPF salary calculators (assessing actual take-home at different offer levels)

### Emotional State

**High emotional investment, limited control.** The waiting post-interview is worse than the pre-callback waiting because the stakes are higher — the candidate has now met the team, imagines working there, and has formed an emotional attachment to the outcome. Rejection at this stage is significantly more demoralizing than a no-callback.

**Negotiation anxiety** is high, especially for fresh graduates who have never negotiated salary before and fear that any counter-offer will result in the offer being rescinded (it almost never does, but the fear is real).

### Biggest Pain

**Salary negotiation is completely underserved.** Candidates negotiate from a position of information asymmetry — they know roughly what the market pays (Glassdoor/NodeFlair is directional but noisy) but do not know whether this specific offer is competitive, how much room there is to negotiate, and what the risk of negotiating is for this specific company type.

### What Is Missing (Underserved)

- **Offer evaluation tool**: "This offer is X base + Y bonus + CPF + benefits. In the current SG market for your profile (fresh graduate, NUS BBA, 2 years of relevant internship), this is [above / at / below] the 50th percentile." NodeFlair does this partially; no tool does it with contextual advice on whether to negotiate and how.
- **SG-specific negotiation scripts**: "For a GLC offer, here is how to negotiate professionally without being perceived as aggressive." This is culture-specific knowledge that generic negotiation guides get wrong for Singapore.
- **Pipeline management during post-interview**: Candidates who are in final-round negotiations at one company while still in mid-process with others need to coordinate timing (requesting extension on an offer while waiting for another) — a common scenario with no structured guidance.

### Time Spent at This Stage

**1–3 weeks** per opportunity (post-interview to offer/rejection decision).

## Stage 12: Offer / Rejection — The Resolution

### What the User Is Doing

**Accepting an offer**: Evaluating it, negotiating if appropriate, and committing. For most candidates who reach this stage, this is a moment of relief and transition. The job search ends; onboarding begins.

**Rejection**: Processing it, extracting any learning, and re-entering the cycle. Most candidates at this stage are simultaneously in earlier stages of the process with other companies — which partially buffers the emotional impact of individual rejections.

**The restart cycle**: Some candidates who reach final rounds repeatedly without receiving offers enter a discouraging loop. The absence of feedback means they cannot diagnose what is failing — interview performance, salary expectations, specific skills gaps, or competition quality. This is a critical underserved moment.

### Tools Used

- CPF salary calculators for offer evaluation
- NodeFlair, Glassdoor for market benchmarking
- Legal resources (for contract review — uncommon at entry level; more common for senior hires)
- Peer consultation (whatsapp, family)

### Emotional State

**Acceptance: relief, excitement, transition anxiety.** The search is over but a new challenge begins — performing well in the new role.

**Rejection at final round: significant demoralisation.** Reaching the final round and not receiving an offer generates particularly sharp rejection pain because the investment was high and the outcome was close. Candidates in this position often need a few days to recalibrate before restarting.

### Biggest Pain

**No feedback on rejection.** Companies almost universally do not provide specific feedback after rejecting a candidate, citing legal risk and HR policy. The candidate has no idea what they did wrong — was it their technical answers, their salary expectations, a competing candidate with a specific skill, or something entirely outside their control? Without feedback, candidates cannot systematically improve.

### What Is Missing (Underserved)

- **Pattern analysis across multiple application cycles**: A tool that aggregates a candidate's full application history — where they got to in the process, at which stage they were rejected, which companies responded positively — and identifies patterns. "You consistently reach the final round but don't close offers — this suggests interview performance or salary expectations are the constraint, not the resume." This requires data accumulation over multiple job searches; no tool currently does this for individuals.
- **Restart support**: For candidates who just received a final-round rejection, there is no structured "here is what to do in the next 48 hours to reset and continue" guidance.

### Time Spent at This Stage

**1–3 days** for an offer decision. Emotional processing of rejection: 3–7 days before effective re-engagement with the search.

### Three Highest-Priority Underserved Pains

**1. No feedback on why applications fail (Score: 125)**
This is the root pain of the entire job search experience. Candidates apply and hear nothing, with no actionable signal for improvement. This pain is universal, acute, and essentially unserved — no tool provides meaningful application feedback to Singapore job seekers. KeyStone addresses the upstream contribution to this pain (improving the resume and tailoring), but the feedback loop itself (did the improvement work?) requires outcome tracking to close.

**2. No structured JD-specific interview preparation (Score: 80, tied with #3)**
Candidates preparing for interviews use generic resources (Glassdoor Q&A, YouTube) rather than preparation calibrated to the specific role, company type, and their personal story bank. The gap between what interview preparation tools currently offer (generic questions, generic tips) and what candidates actually need (JD-specific questions + personal story synthesis + evaluation feedback) is enormous. This is the proposed interview prep module's target.

**3. Waiting / silence — no application status signal (Score: 80, tied with #2)**
The dark period between application and callback is psychologically damaging and informationally empty. No tool aggregates status signals across platforms, provides base-rate expectation calibration ("applications to this company type typically respond in 3–4 weeks"), or helps candidates make rational decisions about follow-up timing.

### High-Priority Stages Currently NOT Addressed

| Stage | Priority | Gap Description |
|-------|---------|----------------|
| Stage 9: Interview preparation | HIGH | No JD-specific question generation, no story synthesis, no practice evaluation |
| Stage 7: Waiting / silence | HIGH | No status aggregation, no expectation calibration, no actionable guidance |

| Stage 3: Job discovery | MEDIUM | No fit-aware discovery, no cross-platform deduplication |
| Stage 11: Salary negotiation | MEDIUM | No offer evaluation tool, no SG-specific negotiation guidance |

### Highest-Leverage Next Feature

**The interview preparation module is the highest-leverage next feature to build, for three reasons:**

1. **Continuity**: It directly follows the existing tailoring workflow. A user who has already matched their resume to a job and submitted an application will naturally next need interview preparation for that same job. The JD context is already in the system — no new data collection required.

2. **Pain intensity at a key decision moment**: Interview preparation is a high-anxiety, high-stakes activity where users are most motivated to pay for help. The emotional intensity is much higher than resume tailoring, and the perceived value of good preparation is clear and immediate.

3. **LTV extension**: Resume tailoring is most needed in the 2–4 weeks before application submission; interview preparation is needed in the 1–2 weeks before each interview round. If a user has 3–5 interview opportunities over a 3-month search, the interview prep module adds 3–5 distinct high-value touchpoints to the product relationship, extending active engagement by 4–6 weeks.

The second highest-leverage feature — **application status aggregation during the silence stage** — would require integrations with external platforms (MCF, LinkedIn, email parsing) that add significant technical complexity. Interview preparation, by contrast, operates on data KeyStone already has (the JD context, the resume, the tailored application) plus user-supplied story input.
