# B2B-First Comparable Products — How Have Others Done It?

> **Data provenance**: Research from training data (knowledge cutoff August 2025). Company figures, contract sizes, and timelines are sourced from public reporting, founder interviews, and analyst coverage unless otherwise noted. ## Purpose

This document answers the question: has anyone done what KeyStone is proposing — B2B-first go-to-market by giving a product free to universities in exchange for data, credibility, and a conversion to paid? What can KeyStone learn from those plays?

The short answer: yes, many have done it. The patterns are consistent. The mistakes are also consistent.

## 1. Case Study: VMock

### What VMock Is

VMock is an AI-powered resume and career platform explicitly targeting university career centres. Founded in 2012, headquartered in Chicago, Illinois. [TRAINING DATA — as of mid-2025]

The product: students upload a resume, VMock's engine scores it against job-category-specific rubrics, returns structured feedback (section by section), and offers improvement suggestions. More recent versions include LinkedIn profile review, elevator pitch coaching, and mock interview feedback.

VMock is the most directly comparable B2B competitor KeyStone will face.

### Their B2B-First Journey

**How VMock got their first university**: [TRAINING DATA — reconstructed from founder interviews and press coverage]

VMock's founder Salil Pande, an IIT and Northwestern MBA graduate, leveraged his alumni network to access Northwestern's Kellogg School of Management as the first institutional client. The pitch was not "our product is better than the human career advisor." It was: "your career advisors are overwhelmed during recruiting season; we give every student instant, structured feedback so your advisors can spend their time on high-value conversations."

The Northwestern relationship was warm-intro first, pilot second, contract third. The pilot was free. The university did not pay. VMock received feedback data on how well the system worked with real students, refined the rubrics, and used "Northwestern Kellogg" as the first logo for the next conversation.

**Critical lesson**: The first client was won on NETWORK ACCESS, not product quality. The product was MVP. The decision-maker (career centre leadership at Kellogg) took a risk because they knew the founder personally (shared institution). Cold outreach did not win the first client.

### Growth Curve

[TRAINING DATA — estimated from public reporting and LinkedIn data]

- 2012: Founded, Kellogg pilot
- 2013–2015: Expanded to 20-30 US universities including Cornell, Wharton, Ross (Michigan), Tuck (Dartmouth), McCombs (Texas)
- 2016–2018: International expansion; IIT network leveraged for Asia; entered IIT Bombay, IIM Ahmedabad, Singapore institutions
- 2019–2021: COVID drove digital adoption; accelerated to 500+ institutions claimed
- 2022–2025: 900+ institutions claimed globally; Asia expansion including NUS (confirmed via NUS career portal references), NTU (reported), and multiple IIT/IIM campuses

**Pace**: approximately 3-4 years from founding to 50 institutions. 7-8 years to 500+. The first 10 were built almost entirely on founder network + warm referrals from the first 3-4 clients. [ESTIMATED growth trajectory]

### What Product Did They Launch With?

[TRAINING DATA]

The first version of VMock was genuinely limited: resume scoring on roughly 10 dimensions (formatting, action verbs, quantification, contact section, etc.) with a numerical score and brief text feedback. No line-by-line rewriting. No job-specific tailoring. The feedback was generic by industry category ("technology resume" vs "finance resume"), not job-specific.

This is important: VMock's initial product was **markedly simpler and less intelligent** than what KeyStone is proposing to build for Day 1. Their moat was the institutional relationship and the data flywheel, not early product superiority.

By 2020, VMock had evolved to include:
- LinkedIn profile review
- Elevator pitch scoring (video + text)
- Company-specific resume tips (based on what employers at that school had been hired from)
- Interview question banks

This evolution happened over 8 years. The Year 1 product would not have passed a 2025 standard of AI quality.

### VMock's B2B Pricing Model

[TRAINING DATA — estimated from public reporting and competitor intelligence]

VMock sells to institutions on a per-student-seat or annual-unlimited model. Estimates:
- Small school (5K students): USD 15-25K annually [ESTIMATED]
- Mid-size university (15-20K students): USD 50-80K annually [ESTIMATED]
- Large research university (30K+ students): USD 100-200K annually [ESTIMATED]

These prices are for an established product with 900+ institutional references. VMock's initial contracts were significantly lower — [ESTIMATED] first contracts in the USD 5-20K range for the early adopters.

**SG/Asia pricing**: Likely discounted from US rates given purchasing power parity and early market development. IIT/NUS level contracts likely USD 20-50K annually. [ESTIMATED, LOW CONFIDENCE]

### VMock's Weaknesses (KeyStone's Opening)

Based on available information [TRAINING DATA]:

1. **No job-specific tailoring**: VMock scores and improves resumes against category rubrics, but does not parse a job URL and tailor to that specific posting. This is KeyStone's core differentiation.

2. **No outcome tracking**: VMock has no mechanism to track whether a student got a callback, interview, or offer from a specific application. The learning loop does not close at the outcome level.

3. **Western-centric rubrics**: VMock's scoring engine was built and validated primarily on US resumes for US employers. SG-specific conventions (NS experience, GLC preferences, NRIC handling) are not addressed.

4. **Static intelligence**: The product gives advice based on pre-loaded rubrics, not a continuously learning engine. The advice from VMock in 2025 is not materially better than VMock in 2022 for standard use cases.

5. **No interview prep integration**: VMock has a pitch coach feature, but no JD-anchored interview question generation tied to the student's specific application.

6. **Price**: At USD 50K+ per mid-size institution, VMock is not cheap. An equivalent free pilot from KeyStone is a direct cost-saving argument.

### Key Lessons for KeyStone

1. **The first client must be warm, not cold.** VMock did not win Northwestern on a cold email. If KeyStone's founder has no university connections, the first priority is building them — not the product.

2. **MVP quality is sufficient if the use case is credibly demonstrated.** VMock launched with a product that would be considered embarrassingly basic today. The question is: does the career centre director look at a 10-minute demo and say "yes, this saves my team time"? Not: "is this technically impressive?"

3. **The SG/Asia opening exists precisely because VMock is US-first.** VMock's SG presence is real but thin — their rubrics do not handle SG market nuance. A locally-built product with SG-specific intelligence is inherently positioned to win SG institutions that VMock is serving generically.

4. **The university B2B market is relationship-driven.** References travel fast. If one NUS career director tells one NTU career director "we piloted this and it worked," NTU will take a meeting. The buyer network is small and collegial.

5. **Free is not unusual in this space.** VMock's growth curve shows they expanded through free/subsidized access for early institutional partners before establishing pricing. KeyStone's free pilot model is industry-standard.

## 2. Case Study: Handshake

### What Handshake Is

[TRAINING DATA — as of mid-2025]

Handshake is a career services platform that connects students, universities, and employers. Founded in 2014 by Garrett Lord and Scott Ringwelski at Michigan Technological University. As of 2024, reported 1,500+ university partners, 10M+ active students, and 700,000+ employers. Raised ~USD 430M+ over multiple funding rounds; valued at ~USD 1.5B+ in 2022 round. [TRAINING DATA]

### How They Built Initial University Relationships

[TRAINING DATA — from founder interviews, press coverage, Y Combinator archives]

**The founding insight**: career services at non-elite schools were invisible to employers. A student at Georgia Tech got 30 employer recruiter visits per semester. A student at Michigan Tech got 3. The platform was explicitly about democratizing employer access for non-flagship schools.

**First partner**: Michigan Tech, where the founders were students. Free. They built the product around the career centre's actual workflow — career fairs, employer posting management, interview scheduling. Not a student-facing tool that schools would tolerate. A career-centre-operations tool that schools actually needed to do their jobs.

**How they expanded to 50+ universities**: They targeted non-elite schools first, deliberately. Schools that had LESS bargaining power with employers, LESS existing infrastructure, and MORE to gain from a better employer-connection platform. These schools had lower procurement thresholds, fewer legacy systems to displace, and career directors more willing to try something new.

The contrast: if they'd targeted Stanford or Harvard first, they'd have faced entrenched employer relationships, high internal standards, and a "why do we need this" response. Michigan Tech's career centre was genuinely underserved and enthusiastic.

### What Made Universities Choose Them Over Existing Tools

[TRAINING DATA]

Handshake's 2014 competitors were primarily:
- **Symplicity**: the dominant career services platform, built in the early 2000s, expensive, rigid, widely disliked by career centre staff
- **NACElink**: NACE's platform, also dated
- **Homegrown systems**: many schools used custom tools, spreadsheets, email

The comparison case was not "Handshake vs VMock." It was "Handshake vs Symplicity." And Symplicity was genuinely bad — expensive, clunky, and had not been significantly updated in years.

**Handshake won on four factors**:
1. **Free for students** (Symplicity charged universities, who sometimes passed costs to students)
2. **Modern UX** (Symplicity's UX was 2003-era)
3. **Network effect pitch**: "when you join, your students get access to every employer already on Handshake" (this was the true unlock — employer-side network effect)
4. **No cost for early partners** — the first 200+ schools paid nothing

### Network Effect Mechanism

This is the critical structural difference between Handshake and VMock — and a lesson for KeyStone.

Handshake was **two-sided**: students AND employers on one platform. Each employer added for one school was available to all schools. Each school that joined brought new students, making the platform more valuable to employers, who recruited even more broadly, attracting more schools.

VMock has no network effect. KeyStone, as currently conceived, has no true network effect. This is fine — it means KeyStone's moat must come from data depth (SG-specific outcomes) and institutional lock-in, not from cross-client compounding. But it means the B2B growth story is more linear than exponential.

**Lesson for KeyStone**: Do not try to replicate Handshake's network effect. KeyStone is not a platform play. The comparable story is simpler: "our AI gets better the more SG students use it, and our benchmarks become more accurate." That's a legitimate data moat story, just not a structural network effect.

### Handshake's Lessons for KeyStone

1. **Target schools where you solve a real pain, not schools where you want a logo.** NUS has resources. A smaller institution (SIT, SUSS, a polytechnic) may have MORE acute pain — fewer career advisors, less employer access, more pressure on employment rates. Start there.

2. **Win on workflow, not features.** Handshake won because career directors could run their job from it. KeyStone wins when it reduces the hours a career advisor spends doing resume reviews, not because it's a cool AI product.

3. **Free for 200+ schools and still became a unicorn.** The free pilot is not charity — it is distribution acquisition. Handshake gave away the product for 3-4 years before monetizing.

4. **The two-sided lock-in is what made them defensible.** KeyStone needs its own lock-in mechanism. For KeyStone, this is probably: aggregate outcome data that only gets valuable with more users, plus the university dashboard that career advisors start depending on for aggregate reporting.

## 3. Case Study: Forage

### What Forage Is

[TRAINING DATA — as of mid-2025]

Forage (formerly Insidesherpa) is a platform offering free virtual work experience programs. Companies (Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan, KPMG, Deloitte, BCG, etc.) create self-paced simulations of real work tasks. Students complete these for free and earn a verifiable credential. Founded in 2017 in Australia; expanded globally.

### Their B2B-First Model

[TRAINING DATA]

Forage's model is somewhat different from KeyStone's: their B2B client is the **employer**, not the university. Companies pay Forage to host and promote virtual work experience programs. Students use the platform free.

However, the university angle is relevant: Forage actively partnered with career centres as distribution channels. Universities would recommend Forage programs to students seeking employer credentials. The career centre got a service to offer students at no cost; Forage got distribution and credibility.

**The pitch to universities was**: "You can tell every first-year student who wants to work at Goldman Sachs to do the Goldman Sachs virtual experience before applying. It's free, verifiable, and employers validate it." Zero cost. Zero risk. Immediate student value.

### Growth Trajectory

[TRAINING DATA — estimated from public press]

- 2017: Founded, Australian university pilots
- 2018-2019: First US expansion, partnerships with Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan
- 2020: COVID-driven explosion in virtual work; 500K+ user growth
- 2021-2022: Reported 4M+ students, 100+ employer partners globally
- 2023-2025: Global expansion, Southeast Asia partnerships

Forage's university partnership strategy worked because there was NO friction and NO cost for the university. The career centre had to do nothing except mention it to students.

### Lessons for KeyStone

1. **Zero friction for the institutional partner is powerful.** Forage asked nothing of universities. KeyStone will need to ask career centres to actively run a pilot, communicate with students, collect data. This creates friction. Minimizing that friction is essential.

2. **Employer validation changed the game for Forage.** The reason students believed Forage's virtual experience was real was because Goldman Sachs put their name on it. KeyStone's equivalent: if a well-known SG employer (DBS, EY, Keppel) publicly endorsed or piloted KeyStone, it would be more credible to universities than any founding team pitch.

3. **The free distribution model can sustain a VC-backed company.** Forage ran at ~zero direct revenue from universities for years while building a two-sided marketplace. The revenue came from employers. KeyStone's equivalent: if MCF or WSG could be the "employer side" sponsor, universities become pure distribution.

4. **Don't underestimate Australia/NZ as a landing point.** Forage started in Australia specifically because AUS/NZ academic institutions have lower procurement overhead than US/UK universities and are culturally aligned with digital experimentation. SG polytechnics may serve this same function for KeyStone.

## 4. Singapore and Asia Edtech — B2B-First University Models

### Coursera for Campus

[TRAINING DATA — as of mid-2025]

Coursera's institutional offering, launched in 2020, gives universities a licence for their entire student body to access Coursera's full course library. This replaced the per-student model with an institutional contract.

**SG presence**: Multiple SIA universities including NUS, NTU, SMU, SIT use Coursera content in some form. SkillsFuture has co-promotional relationships.

**The B2B pitch**: scale continuing education at zero marginal cost per student. Universities pay Coursera a flat institutional licence; students get unlimited access; the university can assign specific learning paths.

**What worked**: The pandemic-driven shift to online learning made digital education infrastructure a budget priority. Coursera's institutional push arrived at exactly the right moment.

**What failed**: Many institutions paid for licences that students barely used. Utilization rates were often 10-20%. Renewal became about the sunk-cost argument ("we already set this up") not demonstrated student outcomes.

**Lesson for KeyStone**: Usage metrics alone are not enough. If students are given access and don't use it, the renewal conversation is brutal. KeyStone needs to architect usage INTO the existing workflow — not be an optional add-on. Making it the default resume tool for career-centre-scheduled workshops (not just a link in an email) is the difference between 15% usage and 80% usage.

### LinkedIn Learning for Institutions

[TRAINING DATA]

LinkedIn Learning institutional contracts work similarly — a per-seat or unlimited licence for a university's student body.

**SG relevance**: Most large SG universities have either LinkedIn Learning or Coursera institutional contracts. This means there is already procurement vocabulary for "digital learning tool institutional licence" — KeyStone is entering a known procurement category, not inventing one.

**Lesson for KeyStone**: Position as a different category (AI resume + career tool, not learning content) to avoid being evaluated against existing LinkedIn Learning / Coursera contracts. "This is not an alternative to LinkedIn Learning; it is the thing that makes LinkedIn Learning investments pay off when students actually apply to jobs."

### Singapore-Specific: SkillsFuture and WSG

[TRAINING DATA — government programme context]

Singapore's SkillsFuture framework and WSG (Workforce Singapore) run government-funded career development programmes. Several private edtech companies have won WSG contracts to deliver digital career coaching services.

**How it works**: WSG or SkillsFuture issues a RFP (Request for Proposal) or SSSG (SkillsFuture Series) accreditation call. Approved vendors can receive government-subsidised delivery funding. Contract sizes: SGD 30-150K per programme, annually. Procurement cycle: 6-18 months but faster than university procurement once accredited.

**Notable pattern**: Multiple small SG edtech companies (names not recalled specifically without risk of inaccuracy) have built initial traction via WSG/SkillsFuture accreditation, using the government imprimatur as a credibility signal when approaching universities. The sequence: WSG contract → "we're WSG-approved" → university meeting → faster procurement trust.

**Lesson for KeyStone**: Pursuing WSG accreditation or a WSG pilot in parallel with university outreach has a strategic logic. WSG moves differently from universities — it is programme-funding, not procurement. The WSG relationship also generates the "government-endorsed" signal that is extremely powerful in SG university procurement conversations.

## 5. Common Patterns Across Successful B2B-First Edtech

Drawing from the case studies above, the following patterns appear consistently.

### Pattern 1: The First Client Was Always a Warm Connection

VMock → Northwestern (founder's school). Handshake → Michigan Tech (founders' school). Forage → Australian partner institutions (founders' network). There is zero documented case of a first institutional client won on a cold outreach with no prior relationship.

**Implication for KeyStone**: Before sending any outreach, map every person the founding team knows with any connection to a SG university career centre. A single warm introduction changes the conversion rate by an order of magnitude.

### Pattern 2: The MVP Was Always "Good Enough to Show Value in 20 Minutes"

None of the founding products were production-quality AI systems. VMock's first version was a rule-based scoring engine with limited industry categories. Handshake's first version was a basic job posting board with a modern interface. Forage's first version was a PDF simulation with a form submission.

The threshold is: can a career centre director see this demo and say "yes, this would save my team time"? Not: "is this technically impressive?"

### Pattern 3: The Institutional Partner Gets Immediate, Tangible Value (Not Future Value)

Universities do not make decisions on "this will be great once we have the data." They make decisions on "this saves my team time starting next semester."

The value has to be demonstrable on Day 1 of the pilot:
- Instant resume feedback students would otherwise email advisors for
- Aggregate dashboard showing career centre which students are applying where
- A student-facing tool with professional UX that doesn't embarrass the institution

The outcome-data-moat story is important for the RENEWAL conversation, not the first signup conversation.

### Pattern 4: Free-to-Paid Conversion Took 12-24 Months, Not 6

Handshake was free for ~3 years for hundreds of schools before charging anything. VMock's first pilots converted on multi-semester timelines. "One semester, then contract" is aspirational and rarely achieved.

The practical timeline:
- Month 0-3: Pilot commitment secured (MOU, not money)
- Month 3-9: Pilot running, career centre staff using it
- Month 9-12: Semester ends, outcomes partially visible
- Month 12-18: Renewal conversation begins with data
- Month 18-24: First paid contract signed

This is the realistic cycle for a new vendor with no track record in SG. Plan capital accordingly.

### Pattern 5: The Metric That Drove Conversion Was "Staff Time Saved," Not "Student Outcomes"

Across multiple case studies, the conversion argument that moved procurement was operational efficiency, not student outcome improvement. Career centre directors are sympathetic to student outcomes but they are evaluated on:
- Student-to-advisor ratios (can they serve more students?)
- Employer partner attendance at career fairs
- Operational budget compliance

"Your advisors spend 30% of their time on resume reviews; KeyStone handles initial review, so advisors focus on high-value conversations" is a measurable, immediate ROI story. "Students will get more callbacks" is a 9-month story with attribution challenges.

### Pattern 6: Reasons Universities Did NOT Convert from Pilot to Paid

Drawn from the consistent failure patterns across the case studies:

1. **No internal champion**: the career director who ran the pilot left, retired, or moved roles. No successor was seeded during the pilot.
2. **Low student adoption**: students were given access but not incentivised to use it. Utilization under 20% kills the renewal case.
3. **No data to show**: the outcome data was not systematically collected during the pilot; the "look at our results" conversation has nothing to show.
4. **Procurement bureaucracy**: even with a willing buyer, the single-source procurement justification was rejected because the competing vendor (VMock or similar) already had a contract.
5. **Price shock**: the free pilot converted to a USD/SGD X0K quote that was higher than the department could approve unilaterally.

## 6. Lessons for KeyStone Specifically

### What KeyStone Can Realistically Promise at MVP Stage

At MVP (before any real-world data), KeyStone can promise:

1. **Instant structured resume feedback** — available 24/7, no queue, no advisor time. Students submit a resume at 11pm before a deadline and get actionable feedback immediately.

2. **Job-specific tailoring** — paste any job URL, get feedback on how the resume aligns to that specific role. VMock cannot do this job-by-job.

3. **Reduced advisor workload** — "initial resume pass handled by AI; advisor reviews the exception cases." Quantifiable in a pilot.

4. **Student-facing aggregate data** — career centre can see which roles students are applying to, aggregate usage patterns, basic outcome tracking starter data.

KeyStone should NOT promise:
- "Our AI is better than VMock" (unverifiable without data)
- "This will improve your employment rate" (requires 12+ months of outcome data to demonstrate)
- "Singapore-specific intelligence" as the headline (secondary to the job-specific tailoring story)

### The Minimum Credible Product for a Pilot Commitment

Based on the comparable case studies, the minimum to earn a signed MOU (not money, just a pilot commitment) is:

1. **Working demo with at least 10-20 real SG job postings and real SG-style resumes** — the career director must be able to see it process a real NUS Computer Science student's resume against a real DBS analyst job posting, not synthetic examples.

2. **Clean, professional UX** — not a Jupyter notebook or a raw API. A working web interface with a domain name. The institution is lending its name to this; it cannot look like a student project.

3. **Basic data privacy statement and PDPA acknowledgment** — the first question after the demo will be "what do you do with student data?" Having a one-page answer ready is table stakes.

4. **Outcome dashboard mockup** — even if it's not built yet, showing the career director what the aggregate reporting screen will look like ("by semester end, you'll see this") is sufficient at pilot commitment stage.

5. **A reference person, not a reference institution** — even a professor, mentor, or industry advisor who can vouch for the founders' credibility matters more than a product reference at this stage.

### Is the "Free Service for Data" Model Standard in Edtech B2B?

Yes, but with critical legal nuance.

**What is standard**: giving universities free access during a pilot period in exchange for the right to use aggregate, anonymised outcome data for product improvement. This is widespread — Coursera, Khan Academy, and most edtech platforms use this model.

**What requires explicit consent under PDPA**: collecting identifiable student data (names, NRICs, specific application outcomes linked to a named individual). Under Singapore PDPA 2012 and 2020 amendments, universities cannot transfer student personal data to a third-party vendor without:
1. Individual student consent (not just institutional consent)
2. A Data Protection Notice (DPN) that explicitly names the vendor
3. A Data Processing Agreement between the university and KeyStone

**The practical structure that works**:
- University gives KeyStone access to pseudonymised or anonymised aggregate data (not individual records)
- Individual students consent via KeyStone's own PDPA notice when they create accounts
- The "data for product improvement" exchange is between KeyStone and the individual student, not between KeyStone and the university

**The consent framework the institution needs to see**:
1. How will student data be stored? (Answer: AWS ap-southeast-1, SG region)
2. Who has access to individual data? (Answer: no one; only aggregate analytics)
3. What happens to data if KeyStone closes or is acquired? (Answer: deletion protocol, documented)
4. Can students request deletion? (Answer: yes, PDPA Right of Access + Erasure)

Institutions that have worked through PDPA compliance for existing edtech tools (Coursera, LinkedIn Learning) already have procurement language for this. Frame KeyStone's data handling as "same framework as your existing edtech contracts."
